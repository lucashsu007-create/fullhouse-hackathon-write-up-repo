# Nederlands

Dutch copy written with AI help has its own tells on top of the general ones.
Read this before writing or reviewing Dutch.

## Je of u

Follow the owner. Look at how they write to customers: their own site, menu,
social posts, replies to reviews. Many restaurants and shops use *je*; law
firms, clinics, finance and hospitality with an older clientele often use *u*.
Don't mix the two on a page, and don't switch the owner's choice because *u*
feels safer. If the evidence is thin, choose deliberately, note why, and ask
the owner.

- With *u*: "Belt u ons gerust", "Reserveert u liever telefonisch?"
- With *je*: "Bel ons gerust", "Liever telefonisch reserveren?"
- *We* reads more naturally than *wij* in most sentences; *wij* is for
  emphasis or contrast ("Wij bezorgen niet, maar u kunt wel..."). AI Dutch
  overuses *wij* and *onze*.

## Main clause first

Dutch built the way English builds sentences puts the subordinate clause up
front, and it reads translated:

- "Welke tijden u per dag kunt reserveren, ziet u in het
  reserveringssysteem." becomes "Als u online reserveert, ziet u welke tijden
  er nog vrij zijn."
- "Of er op dat moment plek is, hoort u aan tafel of aan de telefoon." becomes
  "Vraag het bij binnenkomst of bel ons even, dan kijken we of er plek is."

The scanner flags the common forms (*Welke / Of / Wat / Hoe ..., ziet u*).

## The small words that make Dutch sound spoken

Spoken Dutch leans on modal particles: *even, gerust, wel, gewoon, maar, hoor,
nog, eens, toch*. Machine Dutch leaves them out, and that is a large part of
why it sounds stiff. "Kijk even binnen", "Bel ons gerust" and "Dat kan ook
gewoon" sound like a person. Use them where the owner would, one or two per
section rather than one per sentence.

## Imported marketing and calques

- *Ontdek, beleef, ervaar, beleving* are brochure verbs. What does the visitor
  actually do? *Bekijk de kaart*, *Reserveer*, *Kom langs*.
- *In het hart van*, *hotspot*, *een reis langs*, *met gevoel*, *meer dan
  zomaar een restaurant* and *kom voor X, blijf voor Y* are translated slogans.
- "X, in beeld." is "X, in pictures". Just say *Foto's*.
- *Onze collega's*, said to guests: staff call each other that. To a guest
  it's *onze bediening*, *ons team*, or *we*.
- Title Case is English. Dutch headings and buttons use sentence case:
  *Bekijk onze kaart*, not *Bekijk Onze Kaart*.

## Rulebook constructions

- "Bij twaalf gerechten hoort een toeslag." A waiter says "Op twaalf gerechten
  zit een toeslag."
- "All you can eat gaat over de gerechten. Dranken kiest u van onze
  drankenkaart en rekent u apart af." Say the one thing the guest needs:
  "Drankjes zijn niet inbegrepen; die rekent u apart af."
- "Direct regelen" as a heading is office language. Say what's there:
  *Reserveren of bellen*.
- FAQ answers take a comma and a full sentence: "Ja, dat kan. U bestelt dan
  van dezelfde kaart en betaalt per gerecht." Not "Ja. Dezelfde kaart, met een
  prijs bij elk gerecht."

## Punctuation and numbers

- Dutch copy uses fewer colons and semicolons than AI Dutch does. The
  gedachtestreepje exists, but a page full of them reads generated, and some
  projects ban the em dash outright.
- Times: *17:00* is clearest in an opening-hours block; in a sentence, *vanaf
  vijf uur* can sound friendlier. Pick one style per page.
- Prices: *EUR 2,30* or the euro sign with a decimal comma. Follow the menu.

## English on a Dutch site

Write the English version natively, not as a translation of the Dutch lines.
Tourists and expats need the practical lines (how to book, the hours, where it
is, whether it takes cards) more than the poetry, and a Dutch slogan translated
word for word ("Asian fusion, with feeling") is worse in English than it was in
Dutch.
