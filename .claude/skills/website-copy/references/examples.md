# What to look for, with worked examples

Real lines from three sites built with AI help, and a checked rewrite of each.
Names are replaced with [brackets], and figures that would identify a place are
changed; the patterns are the point. Every "after" was checked against the
site's strictest source (the legal pages, the facts file), not only its notes:
a fact already on the page is not true for that reason alone, and a looser
paraphrase of a legal commitment is a false claim. Where a line is locked
(legal, regulatory, a required disclosure), or the change reaches past the
words into the layout, the "after" is a proposal for the owner and says so.

These show a method, not lines to reuse. Before borrowing any "after", check it
against the whole page you're working on: the same words can repeat a line
nearby (a placeholder, an hours strip, a caption) or say more than your sources
do. Good lines that share a shape (a semicolon, ", en", "dus") still make a tic
when they end up side by side. Then change the sentence, not the connective:
two sentences, a relative clause, or a question and its answer. A new *want*
or *dus* must be a cause a source gives.

The three sites:

- **Sushi restaurant**, Amsterdam, Dutch with an English toggle. All you can
  eat, a long menu, a page full of practical detail. The page said *u*, a
  choice an earlier build made; the owner's own writing says *je*, and a later
  pass moved the site to *je*. The afters below keep the page's *u*, except
  the karaoke pair under "Warmer, not only shorter", written after the move.
- **Asian fusion restaurant**, Amsterdam, Dutch with an English toggle. A
  lounge with an open fire and tables by the window. Its *je* came from an
  earlier build; the lines that can't change (the privacy notice, the booking
  emails) say *u*, so a later pass moved the site to *u*.
- **Energy-infrastructure advisory**, English, no contractions. Finds and
  checks European energy projects and introduces them to investors. Regulated
  wording on the page.

The symptoms are grouped by the test they fail. They are things to notice when
reviewing, not rules to write by: a page can avoid every one and still sound
machine-made.

## Written for the auditor (fails the visitor test)

- Inventory: every true fact on the page. Keep what helps someone decide or act.
- Counting for its own sake: "Zestien gerechten van de plaat", "Veertien
  rolls", "acht beelden uit onze eetzaal". Numbers that help a decision stay:
  prices, times, walking minutes, "meer dan honderd gerechten" when that is the
  point.
- The page talking about itself or its rules: "No firm is named here, and both
  sides are checked." "De foto's zijn echt." These answer a reviewer, not a
  visitor.
- The same explanation twice. The phone number and the booking button may
  repeat; explanations shouldn't.
- A checklist pasted in as copy. Make each item plain and cut the ones that
  belong in the conversation; turning the list into prose is a design question.
- Narrating the photos: "Binnen vindt u marmer onder warme ledlijnen, rotan
  lampen boven de tafels..." The photo, or the caption under it, already says it.

**Directions as a dossier** (sushi restaurant, "Bezoek ons"; figures changed)

> Before: Metro 58 stopt op vier minuten lopen, bij [halte] en bij [halte].
> Vanaf het centraal station is dat drie haltes, vanaf [station] twee. Bij
> [halte] rijden ook tram 3, 8 en 21, en 's nachts de nachtbussen N90, N91 en
> N94. Op het plein zelf staan 40 gratis fietsplekken.
>
> After: Met metro 58 stapt u uit bij [halte] of [halte]. Daarvandaan is het
> vier minuten lopen. Uw fiets kunt u gratis op het plein parkeren.

Why: the visitor needs one route that gets them there. The trams, the night
buses and the number of bike racks are research; they stay in the notes.

**Parking as a spec sheet** (same page; figures changed)

> Before: EUR 1,80 per 15 minuten, maximaal EUR 45,00 per dag, 24 uur per dag
> open, 210 plaatsen en vier laadpunten. Betalen kan alleen met pin of
> creditcard. Maximale doorrijhoogte 1,90 meter, en er is geen lift.
>
> After: Het kost EUR 1,80 per 15 minuten. U betaalt alleen met pin of
> creditcard. Let op: de doorrijhoogte is 1,90 meter en er is geen lift. Is uw
> auto hoger dan 1,90 meter? Dan kunt u terecht in een tweede garage op zeven
> minuten lopen, met 2,05 meter doorrijhoogte.

Why: keep what changes a decision (the price, how to pay, the height limit, no
lift, the alternative). The number of spaces, the charging points and the daily
maximum don't change anyone's evening. The figures keep their written form
("1,90 meter", "per 15 minuten", not "1,9 meter" or "per kwartier"), because
figures are locked. The line above already names the garage and the walk, so
the after doesn't say them again. The second garage is offered for the height
only: no source says it has a lift, and "Is dat een probleem?" after both
warnings would have promised one.

**Counting what the visitor can see** (same page)

> Before (heading): Zestien gerechten van de plaat
>
> After: Teppanyaki

> Before: Kijk even binnen: acht beelden uit onze eetzaal en cocktailbar.
>
> After: Kijk even binnen.

Why: nobody needs to know there are sixteen dishes or eight photos; they can
see them. The counts were there because they were true. "Kijk even binnen" was
already good, and *even* is doing the work.

**The same explanation twice** (same page)

> Before, under prices: Bij twaalf gerechten hoort een toeslag. Die staat bij
> het gerecht op de kaart.
>
> Before, under teppanyaki: Bij twee van deze gerechten hoort een toeslag. Die
> staat bij het gerecht op de kaart.
>
> After, under prices only: Op twaalf gerechten zit een toeslag. Dat staat
> erbij op de kaart. (The teppanyaki list already marks its two with
> "toeslag".)

Why: explain it once, where people look for prices. "Bij X hoort een toeslag"
is how a rulebook talks; "op twaalf gerechten zit een toeslag" is how a waiter
does.

**The page answering a reviewer** (advisory, "Who we work with")

> Before: No firm is named here, and both sides are checked.
>
> After: We pass your name to the other side only once you agree to that
> specific introduction.

Why: the old line answers a reviewer's question, "why no client logos?". The
visitor's question is "will my name be passed around?". The condition comes
from the privacy policy: your agreement to that specific introduction. An
earlier note said "until both sides have agreed", which is looser and adds a
condition the policy doesn't state. It says *your* name on purpose: "we keep
our clients' names private" would imply clients the firm can't name.

**A disclosure that sounds defensive** (sushi restaurant, footer; a required
disclosure, so this is a proposal)

> Before: Bewegende sfeerbeelden op deze site zijn met AI gemaakt uit het eigen
> beeldmateriaal van [restaurant]. De foto's zijn echt.
>
> Proposed, for the owner to approve: De bewegende beelden zijn met AI gemaakt
> van onze eigen foto's.

Why: one calm sentence says which images are AI and where they came from. "De
foto's zijn echt" answers an accusation nobody made. Whoever signed off the
disclosure decides.

**A checklist as copy** (advisory, "How we check": ten criteria in two
columns, two marked "Gate" in the accent colour, and a "We stop when" list)

The two columns and their Gate markers are the page's central design, so the
copy pass stays inside them.

> Before (the stop list): a seller keeps both the build and the operations; a
> number cannot be traced to a source; an investor cannot evidence the money.
>
> After, copy only: a seller keeps both the build and the operations; a number
> cannot be traced to a source; an investor cannot show they can fund the deal.

> Proposed as a design question, since it removes the list: Two things are
> non-negotiable for a project. It needs a signed grid connection agreement,
> and land secured for longer than the asset will run. Beyond that we check
> that the costs hold up against benchmarks and that every revenue figure
> traces back to a published source.

Why: a plain verb fixes the one stiff item without touching the layout, and
without contractions, since the site uses none. "Show where the money comes
from" would have been a different test (the source of funds, not the capacity
to fund). "Keeps" stays: the source states it as a fact about the deal, and
"wants to keep" would turn it into an intention. The prose version reads
better to some owners and worse to readers who scan criteria for a living, so
it is theirs to choose.

## Written to a template (fails the swap test)

- Stock phrases: *discover, experience, journey, curated, elevate, in the heart
  of, more than just, where X meets Y, come for X stay for Y*; *ontdek, beleef,
  beleving, in het hart van, meer dan zomaar een, met gevoel, met passie, een
  reis langs*. The list is for words the owner doesn't use. If they do
  (*genieten*, *hotspot*), drop the brochure shape ("Geniet van..." as an
  opener), not the word, and leave the owner's own lines as written.
- Lists of three because three sounds finished ("smaak, vuur en
  verrassingen"; "Authentic recipes. Cozy ambience. Vegan friendly."), and
  stacks of sensory adjectives (*warm, smoky, fragrant*). Use as many items as
  are true and matter; often that is one.
- Slogan shapes: aphorism pairs ("Few get in. The screen runs both ways."),
  "not just X, but Y", "X, met gevoel", eyebrows made of fragments ("AUTHENTIC
  FLAVORS. WARM HOSPITALITY.").
- Setups: "Sushi, sashimi, teppanyaki, wok en grill: meer dan honderd
  gerechten." Once is fine; in every section it's a tic. Same for dashes used
  as joints.
- One joint everywhere: ", en" / ", and", or ", dus" plus an instruction, in
  line after line.
- Every section built the same way: kicker, eyebrow, two-line headline, lede,
  button.

**Slogan and triad** (Asian fusion restaurant, hero)

> Before: Asian fusion, met gevoel. Een avond vol smaak, vuur en
> verrassingen. Van sushi en sashimi tot gerechten met een eigen
> [naam]-twist.
>
> After: Van sushi en sashimi tot hoofdgerechten, met de smaken van Japan,
> China, Thailand en Korea.

Why: "smaak, vuur en verrassingen" is true of any restaurant (swap test), and
"met gevoel" and "eigen twist" are stock. What the kitchen serves, in the page's
own words, is true and specific. A one-line hero sub-line like this is a
label, so it needs no verb; a section's lead under its heading does. The hours
stay out: the strip right above the hero already shows them.

**The mistake the first version of this file made.** Its "after" for this hero
said "gerechten uit Japan, China, Thailand en Korea". The page says "de smaken
van" (the flavours of), and none of the fifteen dishes on that page is Korean.
On a fusion menu, "gerechten uit" claims an origin for dishes the sources only
describe by their flavours. One rephrase turned a true line into a false claim.
Keep the source's exact meaning, not just its words.

> Before: [Naam] brengt de smaken van Japan, China, Thailand en Korea samen in
> een warme setting. Kom voor de sushi, blijf voor de sfeer.
>
> After, for a slot with no photo or caption nearby that says the same: Er zijn
> tafels aan het raam. In de lounge brandt een open vuur.

Why: "kom voor X, blijf voor Y" is one of the most used restaurant slogans
there is, and "[Naam] brengt ... samen" is brochure third person; the owner
says *we*. The window tables and the fire are true, and nobody else can say
them. Say what is there, not what guests may do: no source says you can pick
your seat. On this site the photos right below were captioned with the same
fire and the same window table, so there the line narrates them, and another
true detail (or nothing) is the better choice.

**The same slogans in English** (same site, English toggle)

> Before: Asian fusion, with feeling. Come for the sushi, stay for the mood.
>
> After: Sushi, sashimi and main courses with the flavours of Japan, China,
> Thailand and Korea.

Why: a slogan translated line by line is worse than the original. Write the
English line natively from the same facts, and let the page's hours strip do
its job.

**Headings in one shape** (Asian fusion restaurant)

> Before: "Een tafel voor / een lange avond." "[Naam], / in beeld."
> "Favorieten van / de chef." "Aan tafel of / bij jou thuis." "Tot straks /
> op [wijk]."
>
> After, where each heading sits on one line: "Het restaurant", "Fotoalbum",
> "Menu 2026", "Reserveren of bestellen", and keep "Tot straks op [wijk]" as
> the sign-off.

Why: five headings with the same two-line shape and full stop read as one
template. Plain labels for most, personality for one. Use the owner's own page
names and words (*menu*, not *kaart*, if that is what they say). "Favorieten
van de chef" also claims a chef picked those dishes, which no source says.
Where the design splits a heading over two lines, "Het restaurant" would leave
"Het" alone on the first: put a whole phrase on each line ("Het restaurant /
van binnen") or make the split a design question.

**Colon setups, three sections in a row** (sushi restaurant)

> Before: Sushi, sashimi, teppanyaki, wok en grill: meer dan honderd gerechten
> uit Japan, China en Korea. / Onze kaart is Asian Fusion met sushi in de
> hoofdrol: meer dan honderd verschillende gerechten. / Naast de sushi staat de
> teppanyaki: vis, vlees en groenten van de hete plaat.
>
> After (hero): Meer dan honderd gerechten, van sushi en sashimi tot wok en
> grill.
>
> After (menu intro): Sushi speelt de hoofdrol, maar u kiest net zo goed van de
> wok, de grill of de teppanyaki.

Why: one "X, Y en Z: the point" setup is fine; three in a row is a tic. "Meer
dan honderd" was also said twice. The hero drops "uit Japan, China en Korea":
the owner wrote "populaire gerechten uit", and the card also has desserts and
dishes from elsewhere, so "gerechten uit" says more than the sources do.

## Over-cut (fails the counter test)

- Fragments and shorthand: "A clear role." "Checked on both sides." "Ja.
  Dezelfde kaart, met een prijs bij elk gerecht."
- Curt instructions: "Tell us which side you are on. Keep the first message
  short and non-confidential."
- Translated word order: "Welke tijden u per dag kunt reserveren, ziet u in
  het reserveringssysteem."
- Official words: *utilise, prior to, in order to, please be advised*;
  *middels, reeds, tevens, indien, dient u te*.
- Every sentence the same length.

**Fragments** (advisory, "Who runs it")

> Before: [Name] / CEO / She runs the screen herself.
>
> After: [Name] / CEO / She checks both sides herself.

Why: "the screen" is internal shorthand; the new note says the same thing in
the reader's words. "Herself" is the claim the source makes. "She checks the
projects and the investors herself" would have suggested document checks the
sources give to someone else. Only the note changes. Folding name, title and
note into one sentence would read better still, but the name card is layout, so
that is a design question.

**Curt instructions** (advisory, contact)

> Before: Tell us which side you are on. Keep the first message short and
> non-confidential.
>
> After: Tell us about your project or what you want to invest in, and please
> leave out anything confidential.

Why: the same content, but it sounds like someone who wants to hear from you.
The terms say nothing confidential goes through the site at all, so "leave it
out of this first message" would have narrowed them. It doesn't ask which side
you're on, because the form's tabs already do, or say "a few lines is enough",
because the message box's placeholder already does.

**Translated word order** (sushi restaurant)

> Before: Welke tijden u per dag kunt reserveren, ziet u in het
> reserveringssysteem.
>
> After: Als u online reserveert, ziet u welke tijden er nog vrij zijn.

> Before (FAQ, "Hebben jullie een terras?"): Ja, voor de deur op het plein. Of
> er op dat moment plek is, hoort u aan tafel of aan de telefoon.
>
> After: Ja, we hebben een terras voor de deur, op het plein. Vraag het bij
> binnenkomst of bel ons even, dan kijken we of er plek is.

**Clipped FAQ answers** (sushi restaurant)

> Before: Kan ik ook a la carte eten? Ja. Dezelfde kaart, met een prijs bij
> elk gerecht.
>
> After: Ja, dat kan. U bestelt dan van dezelfde kaart en betaalt per gerecht.

Why: a yes/no question gets *Ja,* or *Nee,* and a full sentence. An open
question ("Waar kan ik parkeren?") starts with the answer itself.

**Staff talk** (sushi restaurant)

> Before: Onze collega's helpen u graag kiezen.
>
> After: Twijfelt u? Onze bediening helpt u graag kiezen.

Why: "collega's" is what staff call each other. To a guest they're the
bediening, or simply "we".

## Small words that claim

Each of these came from a rewrite, not the original page, and was caught in
review. They are the slips to check for in your own lines. (Figures changed.)

> Before: Bestel online bij [Naam], het Asian fusion restaurant in Amsterdam.
>
> After: Bestel online bij [Naam], een Asian fusion restaurant in Amsterdam.

Why: *het* says the only one. Each page writes its own meta description, and
the first fix caught one page and missed the next.

> Before: De dichtstbijzijnde garage is [garage], op twee minuten lopen.
>
> After: [Garage] ligt op twee minuten lopen.

Why: a fragment was given a verb and picked up a comparison the facts file
doesn't hold. The writer's own map research supported it, but that is not a
source the page can publish. Give it a neutral verb.

> Before (party-size error): Kies een aantal van 1 tot 10 om online te
> reserveren.
>
> After: Kies een aantal van 1 tot en met 10 om online te reserveren.

> Before (same-day note, English): You can book online up to 60 minutes
> beforehand.
>
> After: You can book online at least 60 minutes ahead.

Why: *tot* can exclude 10, and "up to 60 minutes beforehand" reads as a
maximum, while the code enforces a minimum. Read the limit in the code (the
constant, the options in the list) before you word it.

> Before (button under a sent request): Nog een tafel reserveren
>
> After: Nog een verzoek versturen

Why: the first request isn't confirmed yet, and the old label said it was.

> Before (error): Het versturen is niet gelukt, dus controleer uw gegevens en
> probeer het opnieuw.
>
> After: Het versturen is niet gelukt, maar u kunt het opnieuw proberen of ons
> bellen.

Why: the message shows when the server fails, not when the input is wrong (a
reviewer found this by stubbing a failed request). *Dus* blamed the guest.

> Before (under a list of vacancies; a reviewer's suggestion, applied): Hij
> staat er al een tijdje zo, want we werken hem niet elke dag bij.
>
> After: Hij staat er al een tijd zo. We werken hem niet elke dag bij.

Why: *want* was brought in to vary the joints, and it claims a cause no source
gives. *Tijdje* also made a list untouched for years sound newer than *tijd*
did. Two facts with no link get two sentences, or one clause with a plain *en*
("Hij staat er al een tijd zo bij en wordt niet elke dag bijgewerkt"). Taking
the comma out of ", en" is no fix where the joint is the tic: the ear hears the
same shape.

> Before: De foto's komen uit onze eigen keuken.
>
> After: Op de foto's ziet u gerechten uit onze eigen keuken.

Why: the site's text rules had removed "photographed in our own kitchen" from
the English as unsupported, and the Dutch rewrite said the same thing in other
words; the truth review caught it. A claim removed from one language stays out
of the other.

## Warmer, not only shorter

Every other pair here makes a line plainer or shorter. A personality line
written as bare logistics needs the opposite.

> Before (sushi restaurant; figures changed): Voor groepen tot 16 personen
> hebben wij een aparte ruimte, met karaoke. Bel ons, dan leggen wij het samen
> met u vast.
>
> After, in the *je* the site later moved to: Voor groepen tot 16 personen
> hebben we een aparte ruimte, waar je ook karaoke kunt zingen. Bel ons even,
> dan regelen we het samen.

Why: the fun is a fact (you can sing there), *we* and *even* sound like someone
behind the bar, and nothing is invented. "Tot 16" stays as the source words it.
The site's own version joined the two facts with ", en daar"; the relative
clause (*waar*) does it without another ", en". The owner's own warm phrases
(an invitation, a sign-off from a review reply) are the other source.

## Leave it alone

Not every line needs rewriting.

- "Kijk even binnen" (sushi restaurant, gallery) is natural, with *even* doing
  the work. The count after it was the problem.
- "Tot straks op [wijk]." (Asian fusion, sign-off) passes the counter test for
  a place neighbours pick for tonight: *tot straks* means later today. It is
  that site's line, not a pattern; a page read days ahead says *tot snel*.
- The advisory's hero, one plain sentence saying what the firm does and where
  its checks sit, is plain and specific. It's also baked into the social-card
  image, so changing it is a coupled edit.
