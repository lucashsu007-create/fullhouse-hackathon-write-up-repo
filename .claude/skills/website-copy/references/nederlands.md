# Nederlands

Dutch copy written with AI help has its own tells on top of the general ones.
Read this before writing or reviewing either language of a Dutch site: the last
section covers the English twin.

## Je of u

Follow the owner. Look at how they write to customers in text they wrote
themselves: their old site or menu, job ads, social posts, replies to reviews.
A page an earlier build wrote for them is not evidence, and neither is a stock
form's "Uw naam" or a booking platform's *je*. Many restaurants and shops use
*je*; law firms, clinics, finance and hospitality with an older clientele often
use *u*. Don't mix the two on a page, and don't switch the owner's choice
because *u* feels safer. If the evidence is thin, let the lines you can't change
(a locked success message, the privacy notice) break the tie, note why, and ask
the owner.

- With *u*: "Belt u ons gerust", "Reserveert u liever telefonisch?"
- With *je*: "Bel ons gerust", "Liever telefonisch reserveren?"
- *We* reads more naturally than *wij* in most sentences; *wij* is for
  emphasis or contrast ("Wij bezorgen niet, maar u kunt wel..."). AI Dutch
  overuses *wij* and *onze*. The owner's own lines keep their *wij*.

These are samples, not anyone's own words: don't credit them to an owner.

## Main clause first

Dutch built the way English builds sentences puts the subordinate clause up
front, and it reads translated:

- "Welke tijden u per dag kunt reserveren, ziet u in het
  reserveringssysteem." becomes "Als u online reserveert, ziet u welke tijden
  er nog vrij zijn."
- "Of er op dat moment plek is, hoort u aan tafel of aan de telefoon." becomes
  "Vraag het bij binnenkomst of bel ons even, dan kijken we of er plek is."

The scanner flags the common forms (*Welke / Of / Wat / Hoe ..., ziet u*).

## The small words that make Dutch sound spoken

Spoken Dutch leans on modal particles: *even, gerust, wel, gewoon, maar, hoor,
nog, eens, toch*. Machine Dutch leaves them out, and that is a large part of
why it sounds stiff. "Kijk even binnen", "Bel ons gerust" and "Dat kan ook
gewoon" sound like a person. Use one only where you would say it aloud, and
never add one to reach a count: particles added to fill a quota are a new tell.

## Imported marketing and calques

- *Ontdek, beleef, ervaar, beleving* are brochure verbs. What does the visitor
  actually do? *Bekijk de kaart*, *Reserveer*, *Kom langs*.
- *In het hart van*, *hotspot*, *een reis langs*, *met gevoel*, *meer dan
  zomaar een restaurant* and *kom voor X, blijf voor Y* are translated slogans.
  When one is the owner's own word (*hotspot*, *genieten*), keep their line and
  propose a rewrite rather than cutting it.
- "X, in beeld." is "X, in pictures". Just say *Foto's*.
- *Onze collega's*, said to guests: staff call each other that. To a guest
  it's *onze bediening*, *ons team*, or *we*.
- Title Case is English. Dutch headings and buttons use sentence case:
  *Bekijk onze kaart*, not *Bekijk Onze Kaart*.
- *Tot straks* means later today. It fits a place people pick for tonight; a
  page read days ahead says *tot snel* or *tot ziens*.
- Calques run from English too. *Binnen all you can eat* is English "within" in
  Dutch words; a waiter says a dish *valt onder all you can eat*.

## Rulebook constructions

- "Bij twaalf gerechten hoort een toeslag." A waiter says "Op twaalf gerechten
  zit een toeslag."
- "All you can eat gaat over de gerechten. Dranken kiest u van onze
  drankenkaart en rekent u apart af." Say the one thing the guest needs:
  "Drankjes rekent u apart af."
- "Direct regelen" as a heading is office language. Say what's there:
  *Reserveren of bellen*.
- A yes/no FAQ answer takes *Ja,* or *Nee,* and a full sentence: "Ja, dat kan.
  U bestelt dan van dezelfde kaart en betaalt per gerecht." Not "Ja. Dezelfde
  kaart, met een prijs bij elk gerecht." An open question starts with the
  answer itself.

## Punctuation and numbers

- Dutch copy uses fewer colons and semicolons than AI Dutch does. The
  gedachtestreepje exists, but a page full of them reads generated, and some
  projects ban the em dash outright.
- Times: *17:00* is clearest in an opening-hours block; in a sentence, *vanaf
  vijf uur* can sound friendlier. Pick one style per page.
- Prices: *EUR 3,50* or the euro sign with a decimal comma. Follow the menu.
- Ratings: a bare "4,6" reads as a school mark out of ten, and a failing one.
  Write "4,6 van de 5 sterren", with a decimal comma here too.
- Limits: *tot 10* can mean up to but not including 10. When the limit is
  included, write *tot en met 10* (*t/m 10* in a label). In the English twin,
  "up to" is a maximum, so a minimum is "at least 60 minutes ahead". Check each
  limit against the code (the constant, the options) and read it beside the
  options and messages around it.
- *Een*, not *het*, before a category in a description: "het Asian fusion
  restaurant in Amsterdam" says it is the only one.
- Places take the preposition locals use: *op Texel*, *in de Jordaan*, *aan
  het plein*. Pick one per place and keep it across the page.

## English on a Dutch site

Write the English version natively, not as a translation of the Dutch lines.
Tourists and expats need the practical lines (how to book, the hours, where it
is, whether it takes cards) more than the poetry, and a Dutch slogan translated
word for word ("Asian fusion, with feeling") is worse in English than it was in
Dutch.

Native still means the same claims. Check each twin for meaning, not only for
tone: a native-sounding line can say something else ("at your table" for *bij
jou thuis* on a delivery page). Then read the English without the Dutch beside
it and ask whether a native speaker would say it. Where the owner already has
English for the same idea (their old site, a bio, the menu), use theirs before
writing a fresh line. Calques and other non-native English seen on real sites:

| Dutch on the page | Non-native English | Native English |
|---|---|---|
| de kaart | the card | the menu ("credit card" is fine) |
| in de hoofdrol | in the lead | with a focus on |
| dan regelen we het samen | we'll arrange it together | we'll sort it out with you |
| Hoger dan 2 meter? | Taller than 2 metres? | Is your car over 2 metres high? |
| Je eet bij ons aan tafel | You eat with us | You can eat with us |
| van binnen | from the inside | inside the restaurant |
| Kan ik afhalen? | Can I take away? | Can I order takeaway? |
| Wat blijft liggen, kunnen we in rekening brengen | Remainders can be charged | We may charge for leftovers |

In English, a street with a house number takes "at" ("at [street] 6"), not
"on", and a compound adjective before a noun takes a hyphen ("a two-minute
walk"). British English says "car park", not "parking garage".
