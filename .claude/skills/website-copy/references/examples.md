# Worked examples

Real lines from three sites built with AI help, and what they became. Names are
replaced with [brackets]; the patterns are the point. Every "after" uses only
facts that were already on the page. Where a line is locked (legal, regulatory,
a required disclosure), or the change reaches past the words into the layout,
the "after" is a proposal for the owner and says so.

These show a method, not lines to reuse. Before borrowing any "after", check it
against the whole page you're working on: the same words can repeat a line
nearby (a placeholder, an hours strip) or say more than your sources do.

The three sites:

- **Sushi restaurant**, Amsterdam, Dutch, formal *u*. All you can eat, a long
  menu, a page full of practical detail.
- **Asian fusion restaurant**, Amsterdam, Dutch with an English toggle,
  informal *je*. A lounge with an open fire, tables by the window, open since
  2010.
- **Energy-infrastructure advisory**, English. Finds and checks European
  energy projects and introduces them to investors. Regulated wording on the
  page.

## Written for the auditor

**Directions as a dossier** (sushi restaurant, "Bezoek ons")

> Before: Metro 52 stopt op vier minuten lopen, bij De Pijp en bij
> Vijzelgracht. Vanaf Amsterdam Centraal is dat drie haltes richting Zuid,
> vanaf station Zuid twee. Bij Vijzelgracht rijden ook tram 1, 7 en 19, en 's
> nachts de nachtbussen N82, N83 en N88. Op het plein zelf staan 44 gratis
> fietsplekken.
>
> After: Met metro 52 stapt u uit bij De Pijp of Vijzelgracht; daarvandaan is
> het vier minuten lopen. Uw fiets kunt u gratis op het plein parkeren.

Why: the visitor needs one route that gets them there. The trams, the night
buses and the number of bike racks are research; they stay in the notes.

**Parking as a spec sheet** (same page)

> Before: EUR 2,30 per 15 minuten, maximaal EUR 60,00 per dag, 24 uur per dag
> open, 259 plaatsen en zes laadpunten. Betalen kan alleen met pin of
> creditcard. Maximale doorrijhoogte 2,00 meter, en er is geen lift.
>
> After: De garage ligt op twee minuten lopen en kost EUR 2,30 per kwartier,
> alleen met pin of creditcard. Let op: de inrit is maximaal 2 meter hoog en er
> is geen lift. Is dat een probleem, dan kunt u terecht in een tweede garage op
> zeven minuten lopen, met een lift en 2,10 meter doorrijhoogte.

Why: keep what changes a decision (the price, how to pay, the height limit, no
lift, the alternative). The number of spaces, the charging points and the daily
maximum don't change anyone's evening.

**Counting what the visitor can see** (same page)

> Before (heading): Zestien gerechten van de plaat
>
> After: Teppanyaki

> Before: Kijk even binnen: acht beelden uit onze eetzaal en cocktailbar.
>
> After: Kijk even binnen.

Why: nobody needs to know there are sixteen dishes or eight photos; they can
see them. The counts were there because they were true. "Kijk even binnen" was
already good, and *even* is doing the work.

**The same explanation twice** (same page)

> Before, under prices: Bij twaalf gerechten hoort een toeslag. Die staat bij
> het gerecht op de kaart.
>
> Before, under teppanyaki: Bij twee van deze gerechten hoort een toeslag. Die
> staat bij het gerecht op de kaart.
>
> After, under prices only: Op twaalf gerechten zit een toeslag; dat staat
> erbij op de kaart. (The teppanyaki list already marks its two with
> "toeslag".)

Why: explain it once, where people look for prices. "Bij X hoort een toeslag"
is how a rulebook talks; "op twaalf gerechten zit een toeslag" is how a waiter
does.

**The page answering a reviewer** (advisory, "Who we work with")

> Before: No firm is named here, and both sides are checked.
>
> After: We don't pass on your name until both sides have agreed to an
> introduction.

Why: the old line answers a reviewer's question, "why no client logos?". The
visitor's question is "will my name be passed around?". The new line comes
from the project's own notes, so it adds no claim. It says *your* name on
purpose: "we keep our clients' names private" would imply clients the firm
can't name, which its notes rule out.

**A disclosure that sounds defensive** (sushi restaurant, footer; a required
disclosure, so this is a proposal)

> Before: Bewegende sfeerbeelden op deze site zijn met AI gemaakt uit het eigen
> beeldmateriaal van [restaurant]. De foto's zijn echt.
>
> Proposed, for the owner to approve: De bewegende beelden zijn met AI gemaakt
> van onze eigen foto's.

Why: one calm sentence says which images are AI and where they came from. "De
foto's zijn echt" answers an accusation nobody made. Whoever signed off the
disclosure decides.

**A checklist as copy** (advisory, "How we check": ten criteria in two
columns, two marked "Gate" in the accent colour, and a "We stop when" list)

The two columns and their Gate markers are the page's central design, so the
copy pass stays inside them.

> Before (the stop list): a seller keeps both the build and the operations; a
> number cannot be traced to a source; an investor cannot evidence the money.
>
> After, copy only: a seller wants to keep both the build and the operations; a
> number can't be traced to a source; an investor can't show where the money
> comes from.

> Proposed as a design question, since it removes the list: Two things are
> non-negotiable for a project. It needs a signed grid connection agreement,
> and land secured for longer than the asset will run. Beyond that we check
> that the costs hold up against benchmarks and that every revenue figure
> traces back to a published source.

Why: plain verbs fix the stop list without touching the layout. The prose
version reads better to some owners and worse to readers who scan criteria for
a living, so it is theirs to choose.

## Written to a template

**Slogan and triad** (Asian fusion restaurant, hero)

> Before: Asian fusion, met gevoel. Een avond vol smaak, vuur en
> verrassingen. Van sushi en sashimi tot gerechten met een eigen
> [naam]-twist.
>
> After: Van sushi en sashimi tot hoofdgerechten, met de smaken van Japan,
> China, Thailand en Korea.

Why: "smaak, vuur en verrassingen" is true of any restaurant (swap test), and
"met gevoel" and "eigen twist" are stock. What the kitchen serves, in the page's
own words, is true and specific. The founding year and the hours stay out:
the strip right above the hero already shows them.

**The mistake the first version of this file made.** Its "after" for this hero
said "gerechten uit Japan, China, Thailand en Korea". The page says "de smaken
van" (the flavours of), and none of the fifteen dishes on the menu is Korean.
One rephrase turned a true line into a false claim. Keep the source's exact
meaning, not just its words.

> Before: [Naam] brengt de smaken van Japan, China, Thailand en Korea samen in
> een warme setting. Kom voor de sushi, blijf voor de sfeer.
>
> After: [Naam] brengt de smaken van Japan, China, Thailand en Korea samen.
> Er zijn tafels aan het raam, en in de lounge brandt een open vuur.

Why: "kom voor X, blijf voor Y" is one of the most used restaurant slogans
there is. The window tables and the fire are in the page's own photos, so they
are true, and nobody else can say them. Say what is there, not what guests may
do: no source says you can pick your seat or eat in the lounge.

**The same slogans in English** (same site, English toggle)

> Before: Asian fusion, with feeling. Come for the sushi, stay for the mood.
>
> After: Sushi, sashimi and main courses with the flavours of Japan, China,
> Thailand and Korea.

Why: a slogan translated line by line is worse than the original. Write the
English line natively from the same facts, and let the page's hours strip do
its job.

**Headings in one shape** (Asian fusion restaurant)

> Before: "Een tafel voor / een lange avond." "[Naam], / in beeld."
> "Favorieten van / de chef." "Aan tafel of / bij jou thuis." "Tot straks /
> op [wijk]."
>
> After: "Het restaurant", "Foto's", "Van de kaart", "Reserveren of
> bestellen", and keep "Tot straks op [wijk]" as the sign-off.

Why: five headings with the same two-line shape and full stop read as one
template. Plain labels for most, personality for one. "Favorieten van de chef"
also claims a chef picked those dishes, which no source says.

**Colon setups, three sections in a row** (sushi restaurant)

> Before: Sushi, sashimi, teppanyaki, wok en grill: meer dan honderd gerechten
> uit Japan, China en Korea. / Onze kaart is Asian Fusion met sushi in de
> hoofdrol: meer dan honderd verschillende gerechten. / Naast de sushi staat de
> teppanyaki: vis, vlees en groenten van de hete plaat.
>
> After (hero): Meer dan honderd gerechten uit Japan, China en Korea, van sushi
> en sashimi tot wok en grill.
>
> After (menu intro): Sushi speelt de hoofdrol, maar u kiest net zo goed van de
> wok, de grill of de teppanyaki.

Why: one "X, Y en Z: the point" setup is fine; three in a row is a tic. "Meer
dan honderd" was also said twice.

## Over-cut

**Fragments** (advisory, "Who runs it")

> Before: [Name] / CEO / She runs the screen herself.
>
> After: [Name] / CEO / She checks the projects and the investors herself.

Why: "the screen" is internal shorthand; the new note says the same thing in
the reader's words. Only the note changes. Folding name, title and note into one
sentence would read better still, but the name card is layout, so that is a
design question.

**Curt instructions** (advisory, contact)

> Before: Tell us which side you are on. Keep the first message short and
> non-confidential.
>
> After: Tell us about your project, or what you're looking to invest in, and
> please leave anything confidential out of this first message.

Why: the same content, but it sounds like someone who wants to hear from you.
It doesn't ask which side you're on, because the form's tabs already do, or say
"a few lines is enough", because the message box's placeholder already does.

**Translated word order** (sushi restaurant)

> Before: Welke tijden u per dag kunt reserveren, ziet u in het
> reserveringssysteem.
>
> After: Als u online reserveert, ziet u welke tijden er nog vrij zijn.

> Before (FAQ, "Hebben jullie een terras?"): Ja, voor de deur op het plein. Of
> er op dat moment plek is, hoort u aan tafel of aan de telefoon.
>
> After: Ja, voor de deur op het plein. Vraag het bij binnenkomst of bel ons
> even, dan kijken we of er plek is.

**Clipped FAQ answers** (sushi restaurant)

> Before: Kan ik ook a la carte eten? Ja. Dezelfde kaart, met een prijs bij
> elk gerecht.
>
> After: Ja, dat kan. U bestelt dan van dezelfde kaart en betaalt per gerecht.

**Staff talk** (sushi restaurant)

> Before: Onze collega's helpen u graag kiezen.
>
> After: Twijfelt u? Onze bediening helpt u graag kiezen.

Why: "collega's" is what staff call each other. To a guest they're the
bediening, or simply "we".

## Leave it alone

Not every line needs rewriting.

- "Kijk even binnen" (sushi restaurant, gallery) is natural, with *even* doing
  the work. The count after it was the problem.
- "Tot straks op [wijk]." (Asian fusion, sign-off) passes the counter test.
- "European energy projects, checked before they reach an investor."
  (advisory, hero) is plain and specific. It's also baked into the social-card
  image, so changing it is a coupled edit.
